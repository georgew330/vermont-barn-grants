About This Project

Barns are a staple of Vermont's agricultural and historic landscape, and one I think we don’t appreciate enough. There was a barn in my life - well, sort of. My great grandfather, Drexel Hines, lived and worked in New York City as a sound engineer for RCA and NBC in the 1930’s and 40’s. He fell in love with Vermont and purchased a home in 1961 from a farmer in a town called Jamaica. The property featured a primary residence accompanied by a large barn across the street. Fast forward to my lifetime and the barn was gone - taken down by my grandfather, Lee Hines, sometime in the late 1970's. There’s something both majestic and imposing about barns in Vermont. If you’ve ever driven throughout the state you know what I mean. The feeling of driving down a dirt road, surrounded by forest covered mountains, no cell phone service, no other cars, and passing by a barn is what comes to mind when I think about the green mountain state.

In July 2026 during the initial months of my U.S. Peace Corps service, I often daydreamt about Vermont, especially as fall came around but the weather in the Philippines remained brutally hot. In my fanciful, reminiscent wanderlust for the green mountain state, I came across the historic barn preservation program. The program was developed in 1992 to help rehabilitate the historic agricultural symbols of Vermont's rural landscape and has shelled out over $5 million towards barn preservation. The program itself reflects the values of Vermonters, our deep appreciation for our state's beauty and dedication to maintaining it.

Having spent my summers growing up in southern Vermont, and my college years in Burlington - the most northwestern part of the state, I had a pretty good understanding of the wealth discrepancies the state faced. The stark contrasts between towns with money and those without was hard to miss. When I read about the program, I was curious to know if funding favored wealthier towns - afterall, it wouldn't be a very Vermont thing to do.

Vermont is an interesting state, we sport a declining population, roughly 78% of the state is forested, outlawed billboards, have the smallest state economy with the 2nd smallest population in the U.S., and are geographically in a relatively remote area of the country, yet quite close to the 2nd largest city in the entire country of Canada - the French speaking Montreal, QC. I carry with me a feeling of gratitude having the pleasure of spending so many years of my life here, as well as a deep sense of pride in being able to call myself a Vermonter.
